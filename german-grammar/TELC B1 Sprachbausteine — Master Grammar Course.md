## How to classify a gap in ten seconds

1. **Article/case:** a noun follows and options look like *der/den/dem* → find noun gender and governing case.
2. **Verb form:** options look like *habe/hat/hatte* → locate subject and time/auxiliary.
3. **Preposition:** a verb or noun is next to the gap → check a fixed pair before translating.
4. **Connector:** clauses surround gap → decide reason, result, contrast, condition, time, purpose; then verify verb order.
5. **Pronoun:** a reference to a person/thing is missing → identify role and antecedent.
6. **Adjective ending:** adjective directly precedes noun → determine gender, case, article strength.
7. **Vocabulary:** all choices grammatically fit → inspect meaning, tone and context.
8. **Collocation:** a verb+noun/preposition combination appears → recall the exact chunk.
9. **Text logic:** two or more sentences must be read together → check whether new information contrasts, explains or follows.

These types can overlap. A connector gap may require both syntax *and* text logic.

## Core solving flowchart

```text
Read sentence + the sentence before
                 |
           Find gap's role
      /noun form / verb / connector / meaning
                 |
       Identify mechanical clues
 (subject, noun gender, preposition, finite verb)
                 |
       Ask what meaning is needed
 (time, contrast, purpose, politeness, fixed phrase)
                 |
       Remove impossible alternatives
                 |
        Insert answer; reread aloud
                 |
    Still uncertain? mark and move on
                 |
       Return after other gaps help
```

**When unknown vocabulary appears:** first decide whether it is essential. A capitalized unknown word is usually a noun; its article or nearby preposition may already determine the answer. A verb ending such as *-en* often signals an infinitive. Learn clues from prefixes (*un-* often reverses meaning) and suffixes (*-ung* often marks feminine nouns, *-keit* marks abstract feminine nouns). These are tendencies, not infallible rules. Do not translate the entire passage when only a grammatical form is missing.

## Chapter 01 — Noun gender, case and articles ★★★

### A. What you will learn

After this lesson, you should be able to (1) recognize a gap related to **Noun gender, case and articles**, (2) explain the required form in simple English, (3) distinguish it from similar options, and (4) choose the answer in a new B1-style sentence.

### B–D. Meaning, grammar construction and new examples

**Rule.** German nouns have gender (**der** masculine, **die** feminine, **das** neuter) and a case. The case shows the noun's job in the sentence. Subject = nominative; direct object = accusative; indirect object or object of a dative preposition = dative; ownership / certain prepositions = genitive.

| Case | Masc. | Fem. | Neuter | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nominative | der | die | das | die |
| Accusative | **den** | die | das | die |
| Dative | **dem** | **der** | **dem** | **den** (+ often **-n** on noun) |
| Genitive | **des** (+ -(e)s) | **der** | **des** (+ -(e)s) | **der** |

**Indefinite articles:** masculine *ein/einen/einem/eines* (Nom/Akk/Dat/Gen); feminine *eine/eine/einer/einer*; neuter *ein/ein/einem/eines*. No normal plural *ein*; use *keine* or *meine* where appropriate.

**How to decide:** (1) find noun gender/number, (2) find the governing verb/preposition, (3) decide case, (4) apply the correct ending. **New examples:** *Die Ärztin erklärt dem Patienten den Plan.* (The doctor explains the plan to the patient.) *Wir besuchen einen Kollegen.* (We visit a colleague.) *Die Farbe des Autos ist dunkel.* (The car's color is dark.)

### E. Confusing forms: compare rather than translate

**Contrast to remember:** der Mann (subject) / den Mann (direct object) / dem Mann (recipient). The surrounding verb or case, not simply the English word, tells you which form fits. Check singular/plural and word order too.

### F. Typical A2/B1 mistake

**Incorrect → corrected:** Ich sehe dem Hund. → Ich sehe den Hund. *sehen* takes accusative.

### G. How to recognize this grammar in a gap

Find the noun; ask who acts, who receives an action, or which word governs it. A verb like *helfen* sets dative even when English uses a direct object. **Procedure:** locate the clue → describe the needed form in English → predict the answer → check all three choices → reread the sentence.

## Chapter 03 — Possessive articles ★★★

### A. What you will learn

After this lesson, you should be able to (1) recognize a gap related to **Possessive articles**, (2) explain the required form in simple English, (3) distinguish it from similar options, and (4) choose the answer in a new B1-style sentence.

### B–D. Meaning, grammar construction and new examples

**Rule.** The owner chooses the *stem* (*mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser, euer, Ihr*), but **the owned noun's gender, number and case decide its ending**. With masculine singular *meinen Vater* (Akk) vs *meinem Vater* (Dat); feminine *meine Schwester* (Nom/Akk), *meiner Schwester* (Dat); neuter *mein Kind* (Nom/Akk), *meinem Kind* (Dat); plural *meine Freunde* (Nom/Akk), *meinen Freunden* (Dat). With a genitive masculine/neuter noun, *meines Vaters / meines Kindes*.

**New examples:** *Ich suche meinen Schlüssel.* (I'm looking for my key.) *Sie wohnt bei ihrer Tante.* (She lives with her aunt.) *Wir treffen unsere Lehrer.* (We are meeting our teachers.) *Ist das Ihr Koffer?* (Is that your suitcase?)

### E. Confusing forms: compare rather than translate

**Contrast to remember:** mein Bruder / meinen Bruder / meinem Bruder. The surrounding verb or case, not simply the English word, tells you which form fits. Check singular/plural and word order too.

### F. Typical A2/B1 mistake

**Incorrect → corrected:** Ich spreche mit meine Freundin. → Ich spreche mit meiner Freundin. *mit* takes dative.

### G. How to recognize this grammar in a gap

## Chapter 04 — Adjective endings and adverb use ★★★

### A. What you will learn

After this lesson, you should be able to (1) recognize a gap related to **Adjective endings and adverb use**, (2) explain the required form in simple English, (3) distinguish it from similar options, and (4) choose the answer in a new B1-style sentence.

### B–D. Meaning, grammar construction and new examples

**Rule.** An adjective **before a noun** gets an ending; an adjective after *sein/werden/bleiben* or as an adverb usually does not: *eine ruhige Straße*, but *Die Straße ist ruhig* and *Er spricht ruhig*.

**Core endings:**

| Context | Masc Nom | Masc Akk | Dat (all genders) | Plural Nom/Akk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| after **der-word** | der neu**e** | den neu**en** | dem/der neu**en** | die neu**en** |
| after **ein-word** | ein neu**er** | einen neu**en** | einem/einer neu**en** | meine neu**en** |
| no article | neu**er** | neu**en** | neu**em** (masc/neut); neu**er** (fem) | neu**e** |

**Important exceptions/details:** after a definite article, most non-nominative adjective endings are *-en*. After *ein*, the adjective may carry the gender information: *ein gutes Angebot, eine gute Idee, ein guter Plan*. With *viel* uncountable: *viel frisches Gemüse*; with plural *viele*: *viele interessante Bücher*. Genitive adjectives are usually *-en*: *wegen des starken Windes*.

**New examples:** *Ich brauche einen günstigen Drucker.* *Wir wohnen in einer kleinen Stadt.* *Das Wetter bleibt angenehm.* *Sie hat frische Äpfel gekauft.*

### E. Confusing forms: compare rather than translate

**Contrast to remember:** ein guter Film / der gute Film / einen guten Film. The surrounding verb or case, not simply the English word, tells you which form fits. Check singular/plural and word order too.

### F. Typical A2/B1 mistake

**Incorrect → corrected:** Der Film ist interessante. → Der Film ist interessant. Predicate adjectives have no case ending.

### G. How to recognize this grammar in a gap

Check whether the adjective stands immediately before a noun; if yes, identify article signal + gender + case. If after *sein*, no ending. **Procedure:** locate the clue → describe the needed form in English → predict the answer → check all three choices → reread the sentence.

## Chapter 06 — Relative clauses and relative pronouns ★★★

### A. What you will learn

After this lesson, you should be able to (1) recognize a gap related to **Relative clauses and relative pronouns**, (2) explain the required form in simple English, (3) distinguish it from similar options, and (4) choose the answer in a new B1-style sentence.

### B–D. Meaning, grammar construction and new examples

**Rule.** A relative clause adds information about a noun. The relative pronoun matches the noun's **gender and number**, but its **case comes from its role inside the relative clause**. Its conjugated verb moves to the end.

| Case | Masc | Fem | Neuter | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nom | der | die | das | die |
| Akk | den | die | das | die |
| Dat | dem | der | dem | denen |
| Gen | dessen | deren | dessen | deren |

### E. Confusing forms: compare rather than translate

## Chapter 19 — Subordinate conjunctions and verb-final order ★★★

### A. What you will learn

After this lesson, you should be able to (1) recognize a gap related to **Subordinate conjunctions and verb-final order**, (2) explain the required form in simple English, (3) distinguish it from similar options, and (4) choose the answer in a new B1-style sentence.

### B–D. Meaning, grammar construction and new examples

**Rule.** A subordinate clause sends its **finite verb to the end**. Frequent patterns:

| Connector | Meaning / usage | New example |
|---|---|---|
| **weil** | because | Ich bleibe zu Hause, **weil ich Fieber habe**. |
| **dass** | that (reported fact) | Ich hoffe, **dass der Kurs beginnt**. |
| **obwohl** | although | **Obwohl es regnet**, gehen wir hinaus. |
| **wenn** | if/whenever; repeated present/past | **Wenn ich Zeit habe**, rufe ich an. |
| **falls** | in case/if (conditional) | **Falls du Hilfe brauchst**, schreib mir. |
| **als** | when, single past event | **Als ich klein war**, wohnte ich am Meer. |
| **ob** | whether (indirect yes/no) | Weißt du, **ob das Büro offen ist**? |
| **damit** | so that (goal; often different subjects) | Ich spreche langsam, **damit du alles verstehst**. |
| **bis** | until | Bleib hier, **bis ich zurückkomme**. |
| **da** | since/because | **Da es spät ist**, gehe ich. |

**Inversion:** If the subordinate clause comes first, the next main-clause verb immediately follows the comma: *Weil ich müde bin, gehe ich schlafen* (not *ich gehe*). **Beware:** *als* also means “than” in comparisons, and *wenn* is different from *wann*.

## Chapter 24 — Pronominal adverbs: **da-** and **wo-** compounds ★★

### A. What you will learn

After this lesson, you should be able to (1) recognize a gap related to **Pronominal adverbs: **da-** and **wo-** compounds**, (2) explain the required form in simple English, (3) distinguish it from similar options, and (4) choose the answer in a new B1-style sentence.

### B–D. Meaning, grammar construction and new examples

**Rule.** To refer to a **thing, idea, or whole situation**, German often combines *da(r) + preposition*: *darauf, daran, damit, darüber, darin, davon, dafür, dabei*. Questions about things often use *wo(r) + preposition*: *worauf, woran, womit, worüber, wofür*. Before a vowel, an **r** is added (*darauf, darüber, woran*). For people, normally use preposition + person pronoun (*mit ihm*, not *damit* for “with him”). **Selection depends on the associated verb:** *warten auf → darauf warten*; *sich freuen auf → sich darauf freuen*; *sich gewöhnen an → sich daran gewöhnen*; *denken an → daran denken*; *sprechen über → darüber sprechen*. *Darin* = in it; *dabei* = with it/there/in the process; *dafür* = for it.

**New examples:** *Ich warte auf die Antwort. Ich warte schon darauf.* *Woran denkst du? An meinen Urlaub.* *In der Broschüre steht viel darüber.*

**Contrast to remember:** Ich warte auf die Antwort → Ich warte darauf. The surrounding verb or case, not simply the English word, tells you which form fits. Check singular/plural and word order too.

### F. Typical A2/B1 mistake

**Incorrect → corrected:** Ich freue mich auf das. → Ich freue mich darauf (for a thing already named).

## Chapter 30 — Integrated Sprachbausteine exam method

### Goals

Classify every gap by word class and logic; solve the forced grammatical items before meaning-only items; check a word bank globally; manage time without panicking over unknown words.


### Teil 1 (10 questions, three choices per gap)

**Pass 1 — quick read (about 45–75 seconds for a short letter):** identify the writer, recipient, subject, past/present/future timeline, and whether the text is formal or informal. Do not translate every unknown word.

**Pass 2 — label the gap:** ask *Is this a case ending? Is the next word a noun? Is a conjugated verb missing? Is the comma introducing a subordinate clause? Is this a fixed preposition? Is it a logical connector?* Decide likely form **before** looking at options.

**Pass 3 — eliminate:** if choices are *meinen/meinem/meines*, first check noun gender and governing case. If *als/wenn/wann*, check whether it refers to a single past event, a condition, or a question. If *aber/obwohl/sondern*, check meaning **and** word order.

**Pass 4 — reread the entire sentence including text around the gap.** Choose the form that fits grammar **and** meaning. Don't spend three minutes fighting one unfamiliar noun; mark the item and move on.

### Teil 2 (10 gaps, 15 choices: 5 extras)

1. **Scan word bank by word class:** connectors; verbs/modal forms; prepositions; pronouns; articles/adjectives; nouns/adverbs.
2. **Solve forced grammar first:** *ich ___ gern* → likely finite verb; *mit ___ Kollegen* → dative determiner; *..., ___ er keine Zeit hat* → verb-final connector; *___ zu gehen* → possible *um/ohne/statt*.
3. **Cross out used words** (each bank item can be used at most once). Do not force every word in the bank into the text.
4. **Check natural collocations and meaning** for remaining gaps; one form may be grammatically possible but inappropriate in context.
5. **Final consistency:** subject–verb agreement; tense; clause word order; polite *Sie/Ihnen*; noun gender and case; whether the remaining bank choices are distractors.

### A practical timing routine

The source document itself does **not** specify an official time limit for each Sprachbausteine part. As a **practice target, not an official telc allocation**, try **8 minutes for Teil 1 + 10 minutes for Teil 2**, then adjust based on accuracy. If you spend more than ~45–60 seconds on one gap, mark it, continue, and return after other clues have been filled. Accuracy first, then speed.

### Vocabulary rescue strategy (when you're stuck)

Look at the 3–5 words on either side of a gap; often an unknown noun's gender isn't necessary if the answer is a connector. Use structural clues such as *mit* → dative, *..., weil* → verb at end, *sich interessieren* → *für*, *nicht nur* → *sondern auch*. Avoid translating the entire passage into English. For every missed question, write **one phrase** rather than memorizing the whole paragraph: e.g. *an einem Kurs teilnehmen*.

### Distraction patterns
